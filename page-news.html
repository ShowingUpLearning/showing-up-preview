The meme consultant problem

Good Consultant, Meme Consultant

There are whole Instagram pages dedicated to mocking consultants (my apologies, I don’t have an Instagram to share these pages!)

There are entire books about the industry's failures, including Mariana Mazzucato’s excellent book, The Big Con.

And every week, another headline appears about management consultancies doing inadequate, and sometimes indefensible, work.

Are these criticisms fair? Most of the time, yes.

But for every bad story, there are hundreds of positive ones. The very best consultants see the world differently. They solve complex problems. They design human-centred solutions. They bring people together in ways that move things forward. They make progress possible.

What these consultants share is a value: Integrity. Doing the right thing rather than the easy thing.

Every decision you make, every piece of work you submit, and every billable hour offers a moment to do the right thing. These moments shape who you become as a consultant.

As consultants, it really is a privilege to do work that influences people’s lives, and sometimes, how entire societies operate. If we do this work with integrity, even if we still fall short, at least we did our best to do the right thing.

4 December 2025

What separated the imaginative editors

The CEO of a major publishing house faced a problem. About half of his team produced imaginative ideas. The other half struggled. He wanted to know why.

He asked a team of psychologists to study his staff. Half of his editors and marketers. After a year of observation, interviews, and tests, the psychologists found only one difference.

The creative people believed they were creative. The others believed they were not.

It is almost comically simple. To create, you first have to believe you can. Consultancy is not often seen as a creative practice, yet it is one of the most creative in the world. You take scattered information, conflicting needs, hidden constraints, and half-formed plans, and you turn them into something clear and useful.

Our research with more than a thousand consultants showed that creative thinking is one of the nine strongest differentiators of success.

Creators look at what can be. They see the possibility beneath the obvious. They notice the gap in the conversation. They imagine the better version of the thing in front of them.

To become an ideas person, you have to believe you’re one.

Idea for the day: Write down ten creative ideas for a problem that frustrates you.

Kahneman, Tversky and the joy of being wrong

The Joy of Being Proved Wrong

Daniel Kahneman said that meeting Amos Tversky changed the course of his life. Amos was already a rising star ... the most intelligent man any person knew. He was sharp, charismatic, funny, and unnervingly clear in his thinking.

Yet what Kahneman admired most was his curiosity. His refusal to settle. His ability to listen without defence and to keep on searching deeper and deeper for answers. To challenge his own biases. And, most of all, his desire to prove himself wrong.

One of their famous working patterns would offer an intuitive answer, and the other would pull it apart ... not to win but to get to the truth of what was really going on. They treated every question as a small experiment. They wanted to know why their first impressions so often misled them.

That curiosity led them to discover that even experts are poor intuitive statisticians. We jump to conclusions. We cling to familiar patterns. And we trust the wrong things with confidence.

One of the problems they uncovered was resemblance prediction, which we will explore in the next article.

2 December 2025

Is Steve a librarian or a farmer?

Resemblance Prediction. Is Steve a Librarian or a Farmer

Have you ever heard of resemblance prediction

Legendary behavioural scientists Tversky and Kahneman once ran a simple experiment. Here is one. Is Steve a librarian or a farmer.

“Steve is very shy and withdrawn, invariably helpful but with little interest in people or in the world of reality. A meek and tidy soul, he has a need for order and structure, and a passion for detail.” Is Steve more likely to be a librarian or a farmer.

Kahneman later wrote: “The resemblance of Steve’s personality to that of a stereotypical librarian strikes everyone immediately, but equally relevant statistical considerations are almost always ignored. Did it occur to you that there are more than 20 male farmers for each male librarian in the United States.”

The mind does not reach for this. It reaches for the resemblance. It chooses the thing that looks most like the thing. And there is the problem. So much human judgement rests on instinct. We trust familiarity. We trust resemblance. When what we should do is reach first for data and evidence.

For consultants this matters. Our work begins with data and evidence, both qualitative and quantitative. Do not jump to what feels familiar. Notice your biases. Set them aside. Look again.

That is the real discipline behind good judgement.

Reflection question How quickly do you rely on instinct rather than sit with the evidence before giving advice.
