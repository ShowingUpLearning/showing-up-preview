Team games and challenges

The newest element of the platform is the creation of team games and challenges: short, sharp sessions designed to be run together. They can be led by a manager or the team can simply hit play and go. Every game pushes your team's thinking through dynamic gameplay that reflects the real world of consulting.

Try a team challenge

Three you can play today

Three are built and running: 9 Dots, Spiral Dynamics and Canadian Doctors. In Creativity, Can You Connect the 9 Dots in One Continuous Move? hands a team a classic logic task and asks them to solve it without overthinking or overcomplicating. What makes it revealing is not the answer, but what it shows about assumptions, patterns and how you naturally think.

In Critical Thinking, Test Your Critical Thinking Against Canadian Doctors starts in an emergency room in the early 1990s, where a Canadian team cut unnecessary X-rays by replacing intuition with four clear criteria. Your team gets five minutes to design its own decision rule under the same pressure. No medical knowledge is needed, yours or theirs. The medicine is just the setting; the lesson is about how people decide when feedback is slow, rare or absent.

In Curation, the Spiral Dynamics pair works differently: two self-reflections of twenty questions each, one on you as a consultant or advisor, one on you in the world. There are no right or wrong answers. Each option reflects a different way of seeing and interacting with the world, and the result spreads your answers across the eight value systems of Spiral Dynamics.

Play first, framework after

The challenge comes before the teaching, not after it. Canadian Doctors is a prelude to the Critical Thinking module Wicked Environments (and how to make them less wicked). It gives a team the felt experience of trying to make a good decision in a noisy, feedback-poor situation, before they meet the framework that explains why that is so hard.

That order puts an unusual demand on whoever runs it. The facilitator guide is largely a list of things not to do: do not name the framework up front, do not paraphrase what the videos just said, do not confirm or deny an answer. While the group works, the instruction is plainer still. Let them be uncomfortable. Walk between groups, listen, but do not teach. Rescue them and you remove the experience the exercise is built to create.

Then the reveal lands, and it is not really about ankles. A single tested rule outperformed decades of professional intuition. In noisy, feedback-poor environments, structure beats instinct. The discussion that follows asks each person where in their own work they are relying on intuition the way those doctors were.

Every challenge grows a dimension

None of these is a standalone icebreaker. Each one sits in a numbered slot inside a dimension's course sequence. 9 Dots is the third item in Creativity, between Creative People Have a Lot More Dots to Connect and the SCAMPER method. Canadian Doctors is the second item in Critical Thinking, immediately before Wicked Environments. Play the game, then take the module it was built to open.

It is also where Part 5 meets Part 6. The manager toolkit for Canadian Doctors is a facilitator's guide to that one challenge: twenty-five to thirty-five minutes end to end, four parts of three videos plus team work, pairs or threes rather than larger groups, with five discussion prompts and the answer written out for the person leading it. The challenge is what the team does. The toolkit is what the manager needs to run it well.

All of it points the same way. The platform is short video lessons, self-assessments, downloadable toolkits and team challenges: energising ways to learn together, all mapped to the Nine Dimensions of the Very Best Consultants.

Six parts, one platform. Built for consultants who have to be brilliant on the client's worst day.
